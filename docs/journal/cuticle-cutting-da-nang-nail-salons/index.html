Cắt da: the skin half of a Da Nang manicure, and the one step to refuse

28 of the 715 reviews behind this guide mention cuticles · 23 of them are praise and two describe blood. Not one mentions lấy khóe, the toenail-corner dig that has no English name and is the step you should decline.

In short
  • Skin work is not an add-on here, it is the manicure. 28 of the 715 reviews in this guide's dataset name cuticles and 23 of those sit at five stars · they are praising the skin, not the colour.
  • The American Academy of Dermatology's rule is: never cut or forcefully push back cuticles. Effectively every salon in this city cuts. Decide which side you are on before you sit down, not with the nippers already open.
  • Say it in Vietnamese and say it early · đẩy da thôi, không cắt, push the skin, don't cut it. One reviewer in this set asked in English, was agreed with, and had the skin beside a nail clipped open anyway.
  • Refuse lấy khóe, the dig into the toenail corner. It is routine in a Vietnamese pedicure, the hospital group Vinmec publishes a warning about pus and tissue death, and none of the 715 English reviews here mentions it because it has no English name.

Twenty-eight reviews mention cuticles. Twenty-three of them are love letters.

Of the 715 public reviews behind this guide, 28 name cuticles outright, across 27 different salons · and 23 of those 28 sit at five stars. Read them and the subject is never the colour. One reviewer at a My An studio writes that her cuticles have never been this clean, across three separate sets. Another, on the My Khe beachfront, describes the skin being worked delicately and exactly as the thing that made the visit. A third notes that neither the filing nor the cuticle cutting hurt · which tells you plainly what she had been braced for. Across the whole set, 48 reviews use the phrase attention to detail and 33 use the word meticulous.

That is the structural difference and it is worth stating flatly. In the US and UK the training standard treats the cuticle as a seal you do not break, so the step is a couple of minutes with a rubber-tipped pusher. In Da Nang it is done with a curved nipper under a lamp, by someone whose training treats it as the core skill of the job, and it takes real time · reviewers in this dataset describe a mani and pedi together running two hours and call that efficient.

It is also not a line on your bill. The 200K a gel colour costs on this guide's price tables includes it, which is why the three-question script on that page opens by asking whether cuticle work is in the quoted price. And it is most of why a 200K set here photographs better than a set costing five times that at home: gel sticks to a clean nail plate, and the line at the base of the nail looks tight because somebody spent twenty minutes making it tight.

The dermatology position, stated plainly, and nobody here follows it

The American Academy of Dermatology's manicure guidance is one sentence and admits no exceptions: never cut or forcefully push back your cuticles, and if you push at all, push gently after a shower. UCLA Health's explainer on paronychia · the infection of the skin fold around the nail · describes it as following an injury sustained while nails are cut or filed and cuticles trimmed or pushed back, with Staphylococcus aureus the usual organism behind it.

It would be convenient to say Vietnam has not heard this argument. It has, in its own language, at street level. Long Châu, the pharmacy chain whose signs are on half the corners in Hải Châu, publishes a Vietnamese article asking whether cuticles should be cut during a manicure at all; Hello Bacsi runs the same question. Meanwhile the Vietnamese nail trade's own training sites teach cắt da · cutting the skin · as a foundation technique with step-by-step tutorials for beginners. Both things are true at once, and the commercial side won.

So here is my position, since a guide that only quotes the dermatologists is not telling you anything you could not have guessed. The AAD rule is medically correct and practically useless in this city, because it is not a rule anyone here is going to follow on your behalf. What you actually have is a trade-off you control: a cleaner nail base and a set that photographs well, against a small chance of a broken seal on skin that will spend the next fortnight in seawater and public pool water. If you are here for four days and going home to an office, take the good version. If you are swimming twice a day for three weeks · and My Khe sand gets everywhere · push only.

Lấy khóe: the step with no English name

There is a second piece of skin work in a Vietnamese pedicure and it is a different thing entirely. Lấy khóe means taking the corner: the technician works a thin tool down into the groove at the side of the toenail and digs out the wedge of nail and skin buried there. It is routine, it is expected by Vietnamese customers, and it is done without discussion because it has always been part of the service.

Zero of the 715 English-language reviews in this dataset mention it. Not one uses the word ingrown either. That is not because it is not happening · it is because the customers writing those reviews had no word for what was done to them, so the sentence never got written. The Vietnamese-language internet is where the argument lives: salon-trade publications run pieces asking owners outright whether they should keep offering corner-cutting to customers, which is not a question you ask about a settled service.

The hospital group Vinmec publishes the medical side, and it is not gentle. Corner-cutting breaks the skin in a spot that is warm, damp and enclosed all day; bacteria multiply; the article describes swelling, heat and pus at the cut, escalating in severe cases to tissue death, with diabetics and people with chronic conditions carrying the most risk. Its own advice is the boring version: cut nails straight across and leave a millimetre or two at the tip.

Refuse it. That is the flat opinion in this article and I am not going to hedge it. A pedicure in Da Nang is worth having for the heel work · 17 of the 149 salons in this guide name foot, heel or 각질 work in their own trading name, and on the pedicure tables that runs from a 250K express ritual to 590K for the seventy-five-minute signature. Hardened heel skin has no nerve endings and no enclosed pocket for bacteria to sit in. The nail groove has both. Buy the first, decline the second, and you have lost nothing you would have noticed a week later.

Saying no in English does not survive contact

The most useful review in this entire dataset is a one-star account from a Hải Châu studio, and the useful part is not the outcome. The reviewer describes a pedicure in which the cuticle was cut through and left bleeding on two toes, and then · having said explicitly that she did not want her cuticles cut for the manicure that followed · she describes a different technician clipping the skin beside a nail open anyway. She asked. It was heard. It happened regardless.

That is not simple rudeness and it is probably not even a language failure. Cutting is the default motor sequence of the job here, trained in and repeated forty times a day, and a quiet English sentence delivered halfway through does not interrupt it. Which means the way you decline has to be structural rather than polite.

Three things work. Say it in Vietnamese: đẩy da thôi · push the skin only · and không cắt da · don't cut the skin. For the feet, không lấy khóe. Add nhẹ tay nha if you want gentler pressure generally. Second, say it before anything starts, not once hands are in water · the skin work comes early, before colour is ever discussed. Third, back the words with your hand: point at the wooden pusher, mime the pushing motion, and pull your hand back the first time a nipper appears. Pulling your hand back is not rude here. It is the only sentence that cannot be misheard.

Best of all, send it in writing the day before. Booking here happens by message rather than by phone, and a line of Vietnamese in a Zalo thread arrives without an accent, gets read twice, and is still on the screen when you sit down.

If it bleeds

Stop that nail. Not the appointment · that nail. Nothing else should go on top of broken skin, and gel absolutely should not: you are sealing a fresh cut under a cured layer for the next three weeks. Ask for the antiseptic before anyone reaches for polish remover, because acetone on an open cuticle is the part every reviewer who has been through it remembers.

Then walk to a pharmacy rather than to another salon. Long Châu and Pharmacity branches are dense through Hải Châu and along the An Thượng grid, they keep long hours, and povidone-iodine sells over the counter without a prescription. Watch the spot for the next two or three days: swelling, heat, throbbing and pus around the nail fold is paronychia, and that is a clinic visit, not a wait-and-see.

And keep it out of the water. Sea and pool are exactly the wrong environment for a two-day-old break in the skin fold, which is an awkward thing to be told on a beach holiday and true anyway.

Weight this correctly, though. One reviewer in the 715 reports pus on two fingers and an infection on both feet in the days after a visit · a single unverified account, disputed by nobody but confirmed by nobody either, out of a set where 674 reviews are five stars and 21 sit at one or two. The risk here is small and real at the same time. It is worth thirty seconds of Vietnamese, not worth skipping the manicure.

What a gentle salon looks like in the first sixty seconds

You can read this at the chair, before any tool is open, and it is a different read from the ninety-second hygiene check · that one is about what the tools have been through, this one is about what the hands are going to do.

Watch the order of operations. Oil or a soak first, then skin. A one-star review of a My Khe beachfront spa in this dataset describes exactly the inverse · an express pedicure with no soak, cuticles worked without oil, and a buffing machine that the reviewer describes as severely painful · and the sequence is the whole complaint. Dry skin does not yield, so the tool has to force it. Watch whether the technician looks at your hands before touching them, too. Sixty of the 715 reviews use the word gentle or careful, and they do not cluster where you would expect: the salons those sixty come from carry a median 171 Google reviews against 117 across the whole set. Busy is not what predicts rough hands. The individual pair of hands is, which is why the name of your technician is worth more to you than the name of the shop.

Then use the price question you should be asking anyway. Ask what removal costs, whether base, top coat and cuticle work are included, and which gel system they use. The removal answer should land at 60–90K and the cuticle answer should be that it is included, because it always is · a shop that tries to price the skin work separately has told you something about itself in one sentence.

The thing worth remembering when you are weighing all this up: the skin work is the cheapest component of a Da Nang manicure and the one still visible in three weeks, long after the colour has grown out a millimetre and stopped being the point. Buy it from someone with slow hands. Keep the heel work. Leave the corners alone.

Frequently asked

Do nail salons in Vietnam cut your cuticles?

Yes, as standard · cắt da, cutting the skin, is taught as a core technique in Vietnamese nail training rather than as an extra, and in Da Nang it is included in the 200K a gel colour costs rather than billed separately. It is also the most-praised part of the service: 28 of the 715 reviews behind this guide mention cuticles and 23 of those are five-star, mostly admiring how much time went into the skin. The American Academy of Dermatology's position is the opposite · never cut or forcefully push back cuticles · so the decision is yours to make and to state out loud before the appointment starts.

How do I ask a nail technician in Da Nang not to cut my cuticles?

In Vietnamese, before anything starts, and with your hands as well as your voice. Say đẩy da thôi (push the skin only) and không cắt da (don't cut the skin); for feet, add không lấy khóe (don't take the nail corners), and nhẹ tay nha for lighter pressure generally. Point at the wooden pusher while you say it. Saying it in English mid-appointment is unreliable · one reviewer in this guide's dataset asked exactly that and describes the skin beside a nail being clipped open anyway. Send the line in your booking message the day before, since almost all booking here happens on Zalo or WhatsApp rather than by phone.

What is lấy khóe and should I let them do it?

Lấy khóe is the Vietnamese pedicure step where a thin tool is worked into the groove at the side of the toenail to dig out the buried corner. It is routine, it is usually done without being announced, and none of the 715 English-language reviews in this guide mentions it because English has no word for it. My advice is to decline it: the hospital group Vinmec publishes a warning describing swelling, pus and, in severe cases, tissue death after corner-cutting, with diabetics most at risk, and its own recommendation is simply to cut toenails straight across. Heel and callus work is the part of a Da Nang pedicure genuinely worth buying · 250K for a 40-minute express ritual, 590K for the 75-minute signature.

What should I do if a nail salon in Da Nang cuts me?

Stop work on that nail immediately and do not let gel or polish go over broken skin, since that seals a fresh cut under a cured layer for weeks. Ask for antiseptic before anyone reaches for acetone. Long Châu and Pharmacity branches are everywhere in Hải Châu and around An Thượng and sell povidone-iodine over the counter. Then watch for two or three days: swelling, heat and pus around the nail fold is paronychia, usually a staph infection, and it needs a clinic rather than another salon appointment. Stay out of the sea and the pool while it closes.
