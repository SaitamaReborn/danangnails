Zero QR codes in 715 reviews: how you actually pay for nails in Da Nang

Nine of the 715 reviews behind this guide mention a card, five mention cash, four mention an ATM. Not one mentions a QR code, a transfer or an e-wallet · in a city whose tourism department set a target of 100% non-cash by 2025.

In short
  • Bring notes. 45 of the 715 reviews here quote a bill and the median is 360,000đ. Sixteen of the 45 clear 500K, which is Vietnam's largest banknote, and nothing in the set goes past 850K.
  • Cards work in some shops and always cost extra. Reviewers record a disclosed 3% on Phan Châu Trinh, an undisclosed 8% on Nguyễn Chí Thanh, and 20% at a beachfront shop where about 4% had been quoted. Ask the percentage, not the price.
  • The QR code on the counter is probably not for you. VIETQRGlobal has been opened to visitors from China, Thailand, Laos and Cambodia and to Korean GLN wallets · not to a European, British or Australian card.
  • The menu says 200. It means 200,000đ. Get the total typed into a phone calculator before work starts, with removal in it · that is 60–90K and it is the line that arrives late.

Nine cards, five cashes, four ATMs, and not one QR code

Search the 715 public reviews behind this guide for how money changed hands and the returns are thin. Nine mention a card. Five mention cash. Four mention an ATM. Five convert the bill into dollars or euros for the folks back home. The word terminal appears exactly once in 715 reviews, and it is there to say a shop in Thanh Khê hasn't got one.

Now the list that comes back empty. QR: nothing. VietQR, NAPAS, VNPay, MoMo, ZaloPay: nothing. Wallet, contactless, Apple Pay, Google Pay: nothing. The name of every Vietnamese bank: nothing. Across 715 reviews of 149 nail salons, most of them written this year, not one customer describes scanning anything.

Set that against what the city said it was going to do. VietnamPlus reported in August 2023 that the Da Nang Department of Tourism had issued a non-cash payment plan for 2023–2025, with the target that 100% of tourism service businesses in the city would be running at least one non-cash method · QR code, the VNPay gateway, a POS machine, internet or mobile banking · by 2025. The article names An Thượng pedestrian street and the My An night market among the places already converted. Seventeen of the 149 salons in this guide carry An Thượng addresses, and 29 sit in the wider My An and An Thượng block. No review of any of them mentions a code.

Reviews record friction, not routine, so this measures what surprised people rather than what shops own. That is exactly why the silence counts. If QR were quietly working for foreign visitors here, somebody in 715 would have mentioned scanning something in passing. If it were failing loudly, several would have complained. Zero is the number you get when a thing is not in the room.

Three per cent, eight per cent, twenty per cent

The three surcharges recorded in this dataset are worth reading in order, because the gap between them is not about money.

At SORA K-Nail Studio on Phan Châu Trinh, a five-star reviewer who paid 750K for a two-and-a-half-hour hand-painted set ends her review with a flat sentence: bring cash if possible, credit card has a 3% surcharge. Disclosed, small, roughly what a card network costs a small merchant. Nobody was hurt.

At THAO NAILS on Nguyễn Chí Thanh · 1,890 Google reviews at 4.9, one of the larger operations in this guide · a customer asked before going whether cards were accepted, was told yes, and then found an 8% charge on the bill that nobody had mentioned. Three stars, in a review that also describes cuticles cut to bleeding. The shop contacted her afterwards to arrange a refund and she was no longer in the city. That is the shape of the problem here: not theft, an omission.

And at a 22-review shop on Thạch Lam, on the My Khe beachfront, a two-star review headed GET PRICING BEFOREHAND. She was willing to walk to an ATM. She was told the card was fine, the person with the machine would be back shortly. The number she had been quoted was 20,000-something, about 4% of her total; the number she was charged was 20%. Her own benchmark, from everywhere else she had paid by card in Vietnam, was 3 to 5. Both technicians get praised in the same review.

So here is the flat opinion. A card surcharge in a Vietnamese nail salon is legitimate, and a shop passing on the cost of a rented terminal is doing ordinary arithmetic rather than fleecing you. What separates the 3% from the 20% is not honesty in the abstract · it is whether the number got said out loud before the work started. Ask card, how many percent? in the same breath as you ask the price. Three to five is normal, eight is high and real, and a shop that will not name a figure until the end has told you what the figure is going to be. On a 750K set, 8% is 60,000đ · the same as the cheap end of gel removal.

The Visa sticker is decoration. The terminal is a person.

At Jini nail danang, 179 Trần Phú, 827 Google reviews, a couple came in for two manicures and two pedicures and left with one manicure. Buried in the three-star that followed is the detail that matters here: they refused our credit cards, the reviewer writes, even though they have Visa logos and everything all over the payment counter.

That is the general case rather than an outlier. Card acceptance in this trade is not infrastructure bolted to the wall, it is a machine somebody carries, and it goes where they go. The Thạch Lam review says so in as many words · the person with the card machine was not at the location. At Tiệm Nails Xẻo Trân, out on Hải Phòng in Thanh Khê, a five-star reviewer's closing tip is to bring cash because there is no terminal at all; she had none, and spends a sentence praising how patient the staff were while it got sorted. At CIAO on Mai Thúc Lân the note is do note its only cash here, in the same review where a technician lent her a raincoat for the walk to the ATM. At 1993 Nails on Châu Thị Vĩnh Tế, the owner walked to two banks to work out how her customer could withdraw · for a visit that came to thirteen euros for two people.

The reverse also shows up, and it shows up as news. A reviewer at Kimmy Nails Studio on Võ Nguyên Giáp lists card acceptance among the things that made her visit good, which is not a sentence anyone writes about a hairdresser at home. At Nails 4U on Đống Đa, a glowing five-star ends with the only criticism the writer could find: please accept card payments in the future.

The structural version of all this is in the web addresses. 59 of the 149 salons here publish no website at all; of the 90 that do, 28 point at Instagram and 21 at Facebook. Exactly one · CHIARRA NAIL STUDIO at 73 An Thượng 29 · publishes a storefront on KiotViet, a Vietnamese point-of-sale platform. One retail system across 149 businesses.

And one review shows what the whole thing costs a shop. A customer at ALINA on Trần Phú opens by explaining that she had meant to go somewhere else entirely, was told that salon did not take card, and walked across the road. She had a cat-eye set and a foot massage, gave five stars, and will presumably go back. Cards here do not just cost three per cent. They move trade down the street.

Whose QR code it is

The codes taped to counters all over this city are real and they work. They are mostly just not aimed at you, and the reason is structural rather than technical. VietQR was built on Vietnamese bank accounts, which is why visitors historically could not use it; Wise's 2026 guide for foreigners describes the fix as cross-border links rather than tourist accounts. VIETQRGlobal has been opened to visitors from Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and, most recently, China, with UnionPay, Korea's GLN, Cambodia's ABA and Thailand's PromptPay on the other end. VNPay, which needs only a passport and an international phone number, is the usual workaround for everyone else.

Read that list against who actually arrives in Da Nang. South Korea supplied 18.63% of the city's inbound visitors in July 2026 and China 11.23%. Roughly three foreign arrivals in ten can scan the code on the counter. Americans at 6.24% and Australians at 5.86% cannot, and western Europe is not on the list at all.

Which gives this trade a genuinely strange shape. 26 of the 149 salons in this guide carry Korean script on the sign, and those are precisely the shops most likely to be set up for a wallet you do not have. The best-equipped payment counter in Da Nang is equipped for somebody else.

The practical read is short. Plan for cash, treat a working card as a bonus, and do not walk into an appointment with a phone and a plan to sort it out at the end.

One withdrawal, not four

45 of the 715 reviews quote a figure they actually paid, and the median is 360,000đ. The floor is 15K for a single repair at a Hải Châu studio; the ceiling is 850K, at CIAO's Nguyễn Thông branch, for extensions with gel polish, complex design and charms. Sixteen of the 45 clear 500K, and they are all the same kind of appointment: hand-painted work billed per nail at 15K to 100K, sculpted extensions at 500–550K, or hands and feet taken together in one sitting.

So the note to think in is 500,000đ · the largest denomination Vietnam prints. Half a million covers a 200K gel colour plus a 250K express pedicure ritual with change. One note is a normal appointment. Two is a long one. Nothing in 715 reviews needed a third.

The withdrawal is where the money genuinely leaks. Vietnamese banks charge roughly 22,000 to 66,000đ per foreign-card withdrawal as of 2026 depending on which machine you use · Vietcombank around 50,000, Vietinbank 55,000 · with per-transaction ceilings running from two or three million at some banks up to ten million at VPBank. Every ATM displays its own fee before you confirm, and cancelling costs nothing.

Here is the sharp end of that, because nobody says it plainly enough. A 50,000đ ATM fee is a quarter of a 200K gel manicure and most of a 60K gel removal. Four lazy withdrawals over a fortnight cost more than a full set of nails. Draw two million once · ten gel colours' worth · on day one, from a machine whose fee you read first, and stop thinking about it. That is a better piece of financial advice than anything else in this article and it takes ninety seconds.

200 means 200,000, and everyone gets this wrong at least once

Vietnamese price lists drop the last three digits. A menu in a salon window writes 200 for a gel colour; this guide's price tables write 200K for the same figure, about eight US dollars. Nobody in the room finds that ambiguous until the total is handwritten on a pad in front of you.

The reviews are full of the wobble. The customer at ALINA writes that her cat-eye gel was “only 500 dong” and the whole visit with a foot massage “650 dong” · meaning 500,000 and 650,000, and if taken literally meaning about two and a half pence. The reviewer at SORA writes “$750k VND”, stacking a dollar sign, a k and a currency code onto one number. The one at CIAO Nguyễn Thông writes 850,000K, which read straight is 850 million đồng, or roughly a small car.

None of that matters at all except in the thirty seconds when you are deciding whether the number on the pad says what you thought it said. The fix is free. Before work starts, have the total typed into a phone calculator, digit by digit, and photograph the screen. Two people have now looked at one number, and it survives the two hours in between.

That is also the moment for the two lines most likely to appear late. Gel removal, if you arrived with a set on, is 60–90K and routinely missing from a quote. The card surcharge is the other. Both belong in the same sentence as the three questions worth asking at the door.

The bill is not the risk here. 360,000đ is the median Da Nang nail appointment · a taxi to the airport, with change. What goes wrong is never the price, it is that the price turns up in a shape nobody agreed to: a percentage at the end, a terminal that turned out to be a person who went home, an ATM run in the rain. Settle the money in the first ninety seconds. The other two hours are the part you came for.

Frequently asked

Do nail salons in Da Nang accept credit cards?

Some do, most prefer cash, and the ones that do charge for it. Nine of the 715 reviews behind this guide mention a card, and the surcharges they record are a disclosed 3% at a studio on Phan Châu Trinh, an undisclosed 8% at a large salon on Nguyễn Chí Thanh, and 20% at a beachfront shop where the customer had been quoted about 4%. A Visa sticker is not a guarantee either: a reviewer at a Trần Phú salon with 827 Google reviews describes card logos all over the payment counter and cards refused at the till. Ask what the surcharge is before anything starts, and carry enough cash to walk away from the answer.

Can tourists pay by QR code in Vietnam?

Only some tourists. VietQR was built on Vietnamese bank accounts, and the cross-border version, VIETQRGlobal, has so far been opened to visitors from Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and China, with UnionPay, Korea's GLN, Cambodia's ABA and Thailand's PromptPay on the other end. A European, British, American or Australian card does not scan those codes; VNPay, which needs only a passport and an international phone number, is the usual workaround. Not one of the 715 reviews of Da Nang nail salons in this guide's dataset mentions paying by QR, transfer or e-wallet · despite the city's tourism department having targeted 100% of tourism businesses on at least one non-cash method by 2025.

How much cash should I bring to a nail salon in Da Nang?

One 500,000đ note covers most appointments. 45 of the 715 reviews here quote what they paid and the median is 360,000đ, running from 15K for a single tip repair to 850K for extensions with charms and complex art; 16 of the 45 clear 500K. Half a million buys a 200K gel colour plus a 250K express pedicure ritual with change. Draw it in one withdrawal rather than several: Vietnamese banks charge roughly 22,000–66,000đ per foreign-card withdrawal in 2026, and a 50,000đ fee is a quarter of a gel manicure.

What does 200 mean on a Vietnamese nail salon price list?

200,000 đồng · about eight US dollars. Vietnamese menus drop the last three zeros, and this guide's price tables write the same figure as 200K. Visitors slip on it constantly: one reviewer describes her cat-eye set as costing “500 dong” and her whole visit as “650 dong”, meaning 500,000 and 650,000. Before work starts, have the total typed into a phone calculator and photograph the screen, and check that it includes gel removal if you arrived with a set on · that is 60–90K and it is the line most likely to appear only at the end.
